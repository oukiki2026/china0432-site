## Your first night in Jilin in 30 seconds

- **Where:** Stay downtown, either near **Jilin Railway Station** or along the **Songhua River (Songjiang Middle Road)** — both are central, and Jilin is compact enough that anywhere downtown works.
- **Booking:** Book on Trip.com and filter for properties that accept foreign guests; after booking, message the hotel one sentence: "I hold a foreign passport — please confirm you can register international guests."
- **Money:** Set up **Alipay or WeChat Pay with your foreign card before you land** (passport verification in the app). Payments of ¥200 or less carry no extra fee; larger ones cost about 3%. Carry a few hundred yuan in small notes as backup — merchants are legally required to accept cash.
- **Check-in:** Bring your **passport** — the front desk registers every guest with the police, foreigners included. That's standard procedure, not suspicion.
- **Getting there:** Show the driver your hotel's address in Chinese (save a screenshot), or order a car in the English version of DiDi.

## Where to stay: two areas, no wrong answer

Jilin is a compact city. Unlike Beijing or Shanghai, you won't go wrong by picking "downtown" — the two sensible bases for a first night are:

**Near Jilin Railway Station （吉林站）.** Most visitors arrive here by high-speed rail. The station area has the densest cluster of mid-range hotels, late-night food, and easy onward transport. If you're only staying one or two nights, this is the lowest-friction choice: you walk out of the station and you're already where you need to be.

**Along the Songhua River — Songjiang Middle Road （松江中路）.** The riverside is Jilin's prettiest face: morning walks, the evening lights, and — in winter — the rime-ice viewing banks are right outside. Hotels here trade a little transport convenience for a much nicer view. If your trip centers on winter scenery, stay by the river. See [the rime ice guide](/travel/rime-ice-guide/) for where to stand at dawn.

What to pick *within* those areas matters more than which area: look for international brands or upper-mid-range domestic chains (Hanting 汉庭 and Home Inn 如家 are China's answer to Holiday Inn Express — the safest first-night bet in any Chinese city), recent reviews written in English, a 24-hour front desk, and — the one filter that actually matters for you — confirmation that the property registers foreign guests.

For the full area-by-area picture — riverfront vs. Beishan vs. station quarter vs. Fengman, plus where to eat and spend evenings in each — see our [Jilin City neighborhoods guide](/travel/jilin-city-neighborhoods/).

## Booking as a foreigner: the one thing to get right

Every guest in China is registered with the police at check-in — for Chinese citizens it's an ID-card scan, for you it's your passport details entered into the hotel's system. That registration step is why some smaller or budget properties used to turn foreigners away, claiming they lacked the right "qualifications."

That excuse is now officially dead: in July 2024, the Ministry of Commerce and six other government departments issued a circular stating that hotels **may not be restricted from hosting foreign guests based on qualification requirements**, and that platforms may not advertise "foreigners not accepted" ([english.www.gov.cn](https://english.www.gov.cn/news/202407/26/content_WS66a2d827c6d0868f4e8e975c.html)). In practice, big-city and tourist-area hotels have complied; a small guesthouse in a remote county might still be unsure how the paperwork works.

The playbook that avoids all of this:

1. **Book on Trip.com.** It has the deepest mainland inventory, English support, and its listings reflect whether a property hosts foreign guests.
2. **Message the hotel after booking**: "I am a foreign passport holder — please confirm you can register international guests." The platform auto-translates it. One sentence kills nearly all the risk.
3. **Be cautious with** ultra-budget chains, homestays (民宿), and any listing with no English reviews at all.

## Money: set up payments before you land

China runs on QR-code payments. Since 2024, both **Alipay** and **WeChat Pay** accept foreign Visa, Mastercard, and Amex cards — you register with your home phone number, verify your identity with your passport in the app, and link your card. The whole setup takes about ten minutes and can be done from your home country.

What to know about limits and fees:

- Verified accounts: roughly **US$5,000 per transaction, US$50,000 per year** (People's Bank of China caps, raised in March 2024).
- Transactions of **¥200 or less carry no extra fee**; larger ones add about **3%** on top of whatever your own card charges for foreign transactions. A no-foreign-transaction-fee card keeps costs minimal.
- Foreign cards work for **merchant payments** (scanning QR codes, in-app purchases) — not for person-to-person transfers or red packets.
- **Carry ¥500–1,000 in small notes** anyway: temples, small vendors, a dead phone, or a rural stall with no signal. Merchants are legally required to accept RMB cash, though small vendors may grumble about making change.

Alipay is the more forgiving first choice — it has a built-in translation tool that makes navigation easier.

## Connectivity: SIM, roaming, and what won't work

- **Local SIM:** Buy one with your passport at the airport counters. Takes a few minutes, works everywhere in the city.
- **Simplest option:** International roaming or a travel eSIM bought before departure — no queue, no paperwork.
- **Plan around the firewall:** Google services, WhatsApp, and most Western social apps don't work on mainland networks. Download offline maps and your translation app *before* you fly, and keep your hotel's Chinese address as a screenshot on your phone.

## From the station or airport to your room

- **Taxi:** The reliable default. Show the driver the hotel name and address **in Chinese** — English names mean nothing to most drivers.
- **DiDi:** China's ride-hailing app has an English interface and accepts foreign cards. Slightly more predictable pricing than a street taxi.
- **Hotel pickup:** Some mid-range and international hotels offer it if you ask when booking. Worth one message.

If you're coming from Changchun Longjia Airport (CGQ), the step-by-step transfer is covered in [how to get to Jilin City](/travel/how-to-get-to-jilin/).

## Your first evening

Don't over-plan it. Drop your bags, walk the riverside on Songjiang Middle Road, and eat nearby — the full food survival guide (what to order, how to point at a menu) is here: [what to eat in Jilin](/taste/what-to-eat-in-jilin/). For tonight: pick a busy-looking local restaurant within walking distance, point at what looks good, and pay by scanning the QR code on the table.

One housekeeping note for the room: **don't drink the tap water** — standard across China. Hotels provide an electric kettle and usually bottled water; boil or use bottled.

## FAQ

**Can foreigners stay in any hotel in Jilin?**
Nearly any hotel that regularly serves travelers, yes. Since the July 2024 government circular, hotels can't refuse you over "qualifications." The remaining risk is small, non-tourist properties that never set up foreign-guest registration — book ahead on Trip.com and confirm with one message.

**Do I need cash in Jilin?**
Mostly no, but carry ¥500–1,000 in small notes as backup. Hotels, restaurants, and taxis all take mobile payment or cards.

**Will my foreign credit card work at the hotel front desk?**
International-brand hotels usually accept foreign cards directly. Elsewhere, pay with your linked Alipay/WeChat Pay, which draws from the same foreign card.

**How do I get a taxi if I don't speak Chinese?**
Use DiDi's English interface, or show any driver your destination in Chinese characters. Hotel staff will also call one for you.

**Is the tap water safe?**
No — don't drink it. Use the kettle in your room or bottled water, like everywhere else in China.
